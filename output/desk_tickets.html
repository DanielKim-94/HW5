Reflection

My observations below come from using this app. The ticket outcomes and delegation examples come from the preserved database, audit trail, and screenshots. The additional use cases are AI-assisted suggestions, not tested results.

What I learned from the three tickets

Ticket 103 showed the most useful analysis. The agents identified that only 8 of the requested 20 medium hoodies were available, checked the recorded cost of 22 and price of 58, and identified pricing and supplier uncertainties. However, their analysis did not independently resolve the request. I chose to retain 58 per hoodie and approved an unsent conditional quote: 20 hoodies would total 1,160, but fulfillment of all 20 was not guaranteed. Closing the pricing request did not record customer acceptance, hoodie fulfillment, or revenue.

Ticket 101 required the most intervention from me. Inventory identified zero size-S tees, and the linked invoice required a human-approved payment of 840. Paying that invoice removed a payment-related supplier blocker but did not prove stock receipt or complete the customer order. I separately approved the classroom assumptions and the simulated receipt and fulfillment of one tee. The simulation used five calendar days from the recorded August 31 payment, giving September 5, 2026 as the simulated receipt and fulfillment date. The assumption that the paid invoice covered one tee was explicitly accepted for the classroom exercise; it was not independently proved by the original invoice. No physical delivery was claimed and no additional payment was made.

Ticket 102 had the clearest financial completion criterion. Facilities and Accounting checked the rent context, but an early run incorrectly treated the future due date as a reason not to prepare approval. After the workflow was corrected, my approval allowed the recorded rent payment of 2,400. The database then showed the rent ticket resolved and the lease's next due date as October 2. The agents could investigate and prepare a request; they could not approve their own payment.

Cash reconciliation: 3,400 − 840 − 2,400 = 160. These were the two current recorded payments, with named human approval evidence. Ticket 103 did not add revenue, and ticket 101's simulation did not add another debit. Earlier payment records from reset generations are historical evidence, not additional charges in the current ledger.

Completion limits: the criteria for ticket 101's simulated fulfillment and ticket 103's pricing-only closure were approved by me, not confirmed by the instructor. A database status of Resolved reflects these recorded criteria; it does not establish actual delivery or acceptance of the quote.

Expected and actual delegation

For ticket 101, I expected Boss to contact Inventory first, followed by Inventory handing the invoice question to Accounting and the customer explanation to Customer Service. One preserved run instead shows Boss contacting Inventory, Accounting, and Customer Service directly. The specialists contributed stock and invoice analysis and an unsent customer draft, but that run did not prepare the payment request or resolve the order.

For ticket 102, I expected Boss to contact Facilities and Facilities to hand the financial check to Accounting. The documented run shows Boss contacting both directly. Its report said it was awaiting a human, but no approval ID existed. This was a failure to prepare the request, not proof that an existing request was simply hidden.

For ticket 103, I expected Inventory first, then Accounting and Customer Service. Actual attempts included Boss contacting Accounting, Accounting contacting Inventory, and Boss contacting Inventory again. Another attempt started with Inventory but still repeated the Inventory check through Accounting. These examples show departures from my plan; they do not establish that every planned handoff occurred in every run.

My observation is that the five roles clarified responsibilities, but repeated delegation and stock checks felt unnecessary. Sharing findings across agents would make the process more efficient. The implementation later added reuse of specialist findings within a run, but that fix should not be mistaken for evidence that earlier attempts were efficient.

Difficulties and trust in the dashboard

I learned that ?Run completed? does not mean a ticket is resolved. Some attempts failed; others ended with a draft or a blocker while the ticket stayed open. One ticket-103 attempt exceeded the 40,000-token limit and reported 43,265 tokens after repeated investigation. A limit checked after a response can be exceeded before the run stops.

Missing approval displays had different explanations. Some runs never created a request, and an earlier pending invoice request was deleted by a database reset. Preparation, display, human approval, payment execution, and continuation therefore needed separate checks. The dashboard also kept an old paid-invoice error visible when another ticket was selected, which made the failure harder to understand.

Later resets cleared completed receipt, quote, and resolution records. Earlier screenshots were genuine but became historical evidence, and the completion report became stale. Recovery restored the previously authorized records without repeating payments or simulation operations. These problems taught me to check dashboard messages against database and audit records, and to distinguish current results from earlier attempts.

The agents contributed investigation, financial and stock analysis, and drafts. The coding assistant's implementation fixes improved preparation, approval controls, continuation, reuse of findings, and status displays. My business decisions supplied the simulation assumptions and the pricing-only completion rule. Passing implementation tests was useful, but actual database and dashboard evidence was needed to support ticket resolution.

Where one agent with tools would be simpler

AI-assisted assessment: a single agent could probably handle ticket 102 by reading get_rent_payment_context, preparing request_payment_approval, and stopping for a human decision. A fixed sequence of checks would avoid unnecessary handoffs. The stock and price reads for ticket 103 could also be combined using get_bulk_pricing_context. For ticket 101, one agent could check get_customer_order_context and vendor status, then prepare the known invoice request. Having five agents did not remove the need for human approval or justify inventing receipt evidence. Separate roles remain helpful when their expertise produces distinct work rather than repeated database reads.

Three additional problems the current team could help with

These are proposed uses within the implemented ticket types and available records, not additional completed runs.

  1. Review another recorded supplier invoice: Accounting can check cash and shipping restrictions, prepare a payment request, and stop for human approval. Vendor-status and payment tools can verify the result afterward.
  2. Answer an exact-size availability inquiry: Inventory can use the customer-order context to identify a shortage or available stock, and Customer Service can use save_message_draft to prepare an unsent response.
  3. Prepare a conditional bulk-price response: Accounting and Inventory can check recorded price, cost, and stock; quote and resolution-review tools can record a supported human decision without claiming acceptance or guaranteed supply.

Three problems the current team cannot complete

  1. Verify a real delivery: the simulation tools cannot prove a supplier shipped or a package arrived. Real receipt would need supplier or carrier evidence, a receiving-record tool, and a human receiving process or logistics role.
  2. Process a return and refund: the app lacks sales, returns, refund eligibility, and payment-processor records. It would need those tools and an explicit human-approved refund workflow; current cash-out safeguards alone are insufficient.
  3. Secure the missing 12 hoodies from a supplier: there is no confirmed hoodie supplier mapping or live commitment. Supplier catalog, quotation, and authorized communication tools, plus a procurement role, would be needed. Current checking of 160 is also less than 12 times the recorded unit cost of 22, or 264; that arithmetic is not a supplier quote, and the app cannot invent revenue to cover it.

What I would improve first

I would first improve the approval and status workflow, clearly separate current results from historical attempts, and add safeguards against accidental resets. I would keep plan approval, simulated receipt, fulfillment, and ticket resolution distinct. My experience showed that useful agent reasoning is only one part of a reliable app: durable records, clear human decisions, and accurate status reporting matter just as much.

Evidence: completed-but-open investigation, post-payment and token-limit investigation, reset and recovery investigation, and current cash reconciliation.

Shared checks and one tool gap before execution

Keep each delegation on its current ticket and avoid active-ancestor cycles. Specialist findings return through the existing call chain; a return is not a new delegation to Boss. Human approval is separate from agent delegation.

Proposed addition: get_cash_obligations(ticket_id), reading desk, cash_accounts, invoices, leases, and payments. This is a planned tool, not an implemented one. Ticket 102 already exposes its competing invoice; ticket 101 cannot currently inspect the rent obligation without switching tickets.

The known rent and invoice together leave 160 from the recorded 3400 balance. This is arithmetic, not approval to pay either obligation; currency is unspecified.

All shop facts and changes must use MCP. Cash only goes out. Messages remain unsent drafts. Paying an invoice does not create stock; confirmed receipt/restock tooling remains a gap.
